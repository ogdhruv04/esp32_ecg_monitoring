— Summary

Where we are
A bench prototype (ESP32-C3 + MAX30003) streams ECG over BLE to an Android app. Both ends work. A defect that silently corrupted every reading has been found and fixed (§0.2) — but data captured before that fix should be discarded, so the accuracy baseline starts from zero.
The core problem
We cannot currently tell whether our pipeline is measuring a heartbeat or manufacturing one. A filter and peak detector will produce a confident, plausible heart rate from pure noise. Resolving this — not adding features — is what the next twelve weeks are for.
What we will build
A correctly-sampled ECG front end; a classical filtering and R-peak pipeline benchmarked against annotated public data; an IMU-referenced adaptive filter for motion artifact; a signal-quality index; and a classical atrial-fibrillation baseline.
What we will not claim
Clinical arrhythmia sensitivity (we have no patients with atrial fibrillation), QT/interval morphology (insufficient sample rate), and non-invasive glucose (the evidence does not support it — §6).
How we verify
Every week ends at a numeric gate. Every user-visible number traces to a line in the validation report. All model evaluation uses subject-wise splits.
Biggest risk
Ethics approval has the longest lead time of anything here and gates all human-subject data collection. It is started in Week 1 for that reason.

Part 0 Current status & the blocking defect

0.1Why a reference device is not optional

A filter cannot tell you whether its own output is real. Bandpass-filter pure electrical noise between 0.5–40 Hz and you get a smooth, plausible-looking squiggle. Run a peak detector on it and it will find peaks, space them believably, and report "72 bpm." Everything downstream — heart-rate variability, an atrial-fibrillation flag, the chart in the app — will look completely healthy. It is entirely fabricated.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the default failure mode of biosignal pipelines, and it is invisible from the inside.

There are two different questions here, and they need two different sources of truth:

QuestionAnswered byCannot be answered by
Is our R-peak detection algorithm correct?MIT-BIH — expert-annotated beatsOur own hardware — no ground-truth labels
Does the ring capture real cardiac activity?Simultaneous reference recordingMIT-BIH — different electrodes, site, noise

Public datasets validate our code. Only a simultaneous reference recording validates our hardware and code as a system. Both are required; neither substitutes for the other.

The reference answers, for every beat the ring claims to have seen, whether a heartbeat actually occurred. Without it, every number the system produces is unfalsifiable — and an unfalsifiable number is not a result.

0.2The defect found this week

Blocking · invalidates all ECG captured to date

Every ECG sample recorded so far has identical low 6 bits (0b111111), verified across two independent captures. Those bits are not signal — they are a status field, and it reads FIFO_OVERFLOW.

The MAX30003 does not return a 24-bit sample. It returns a 24-bit word packing three fields:

bits [23:6] = 18-bit signed ECG sample
bits [5:3]  = ETAG  (status: valid / empty / overflow)
bits [2:0]  = PTAG  (pace detection)

Two defects, stacked

(a) The vendor library never shifts. It sign-extends the full word from bit 23 and returns sample × 64 + status_bits. The waveform shape survives, which is why the chart looked plausible — but every amplitude is wrong and six bits of status ride on the signal. Correct handling is to shift right by 6, then sign-extend from bit 17.

(b) The sensor was reporting overflow continuously. ETAG read 7 (FIFO_OVERFLOW) on every sample. Because the library never masks the tag bits off, nothing was reading it.

Root cause was ours: the sensor was configured for 128 samples/sec while the firmware polled at ~125 Hz. Consumption ran roughly three samples per second behind production, the 32-deep FIFO saturated in about ten seconds, and it then stayed latched in overflow.

This explains an earlier misdiagnosis

Early in a capture, values swung widely — plausibly real signal. They then collapsed into a narrow band and stayed there. We attributed that to poor electrode contact. It was almost certainly the FIFO latching into overflow and returning stale data. Worth remembering: we had a confident hardware explanation for what turned out to be a software defect.

0.3Fix status

Fixed and flashed

The fix is implemented, flashed and confirmed working on hardware. Recordings made before it should be discarded rather than reanalysed — the samples were overwritten with status data, so the underlying signal cannot be recovered from them.

What the fix changes:

  1. Correct decode — shift right 6, sign-extend from bit 17, and expose ETAG and PTAG separately instead of folding them into the sample.
  2. Status is now checked — valid samples are used, an empty FIFO ends the read, and an overflow triggers a FIFO reset and increments an error counter.
  3. The FIFO is fully drained on every service, which is the actual root-cause fix: it decouples our service rate from the sensor's sample rate, so a rate mismatch can no longer accumulate.
  4. INT1 is finally used. The interrupt line was physically wired but never enabled, because the vendor library configures no interrupts at all. The firmware now enables the FIFO and overflow interrupts and services the FIFO on the interrupt rather than on a fixed delay.
  5. Diagnostic counters report the status-tag distribution every five seconds.
Standing regression check The diagnostic counters stay in the firmware. Any future change to sampling rate, packet size or interrupt configuration should be re-checked the same way: overflow events must remain zero, and samples must arrive evenly rather than in 100 ms bursts — bursty output means the interrupt has stopped firing and the safety-net sweep is masking it.

Part 1 How the signal processing works

1.1The signal, and what corrupts it

ComponentBandRole
QRS complex~10–25 HzSharp and high-energy; the target of R-peak detection
P wave~0.5–10 HzAtrial depolarization. Absent in atrial fibrillation
T wave~0.5–10 HzRepolarization; easily mistaken for an R-peak
Diagnostic bandwidth0.05–150 HzRequired for true morphology (ST segment, QT interval)
Monitoring bandwidth0.5–40 HzSufficient for heart rate, HRV and AF detection
Noise sourceBandRemovable by frequency filtering?
Baseline wander — respiration, electrode drift0.05–0.5 HzYes — high-pass
Mains interference (India: 50 Hz)50 / 100 / 150 HzYes — narrow notch
Muscle activity (EMG)20–500 HzPartially — overlaps the QRS
Motion artifact0.1–20 HzNo — occupies the ECG band itself

That final row is the central difficulty. Motion artifact sits at the same frequencies as the signal, so no frequency-domain filter can separate the two — a notch placed on the artifact removes the ECG with it. This is precisely why the ring's accelerometer and gyroscope matter (§1.3).

1.2The classical filter chain

High-pass at ~0.5 Hz removes baseline wander, the slow drift caused by changing contact pressure and perspiration that otherwise walks the trace off-screen. Offline processing should use a zero-phase forward-backward filter; a causal filter delays the waveform and corrupts interval measurements. On-device real-time processing must accept causal filtering and characterise its delay. Raising the corner above ~0.5 Hz begins distorting the ST segment — acceptable for heart rate, not for morphology.

A narrow notch at 50 Hz (Q ≈ 30) removes mains interference, with a second at 100 Hz if harmonics are visible. The notch must stay narrow, or it consumes QRS energy.

Low-pass at ~40 Hz removes muscle noise and high-frequency content above the monitoring band.

Sample rate — action required in Week 1

The sensor currently runs at 128 samples/sec. That is adequate for heart rate, marginal for HRV — RR intervals quantise to 7.8 ms against RMSSD values of 20–50 ms — and insufficient for QT or QRS-duration measurement. Move to 256 SPS minimum. At 512 SPS the current six-sample BLE batch would fire every 11.7 ms, exceeding the connection interval, so larger batches and MTU negotiation would be required.

1.3Motion artifact, and why the IMU matters

Because motion artifact cannot be separated from the ECG by frequency, it must be separated by reference: an independent measurement of the motion itself, which the accelerometer and gyroscope provide.

observed(n) = clean_ecg(n) + artifact(n)
                                  ^ correlated with IMU motion x(n)

An adaptive filter learns weights w such that   w.x(n) ~ artifact(n)
Output:   e(n) = observed(n) - w.x(n)   ~   clean_ecg(n)

NLMS update:   w(n+1) = w(n) + mu . e(n) . x(n) / (||x(n)||^2 + eps)

The method rests on an assumption that genuinely holds here: cardiac electrical activity is uncorrelated with hand motion, while the artifact is strongly correlated with it. The filter can therefore only subtract the motion-driven component — it cannot consume the ECG.

Both sensors are needed. The accelerometer captures translation (arm swing, tapping); the gyroscope captures rotation (wrist twist, finger roll). For a ring, rotation is often dominant, because it directly alters electrode-skin contact geometry. All six channels should feed the filter.

The IMU must be sampled synchronously with the ECG and timestamped on the same clock. Asynchronous IMU data is useless as an adaptive reference. This belongs in the firmware in Week 2; retrofitting it later is painful.

Normalised least-mean-squares is roughly thirty lines of code and runs on-device. It should be built first, and any deep-learning denoiser must beat it to justify its cost.

1.4R-peak detection

The Pan–Tompkins algorithm remains the standard and is still competitive. It runs in six stages:

  1. Bandpass, 5–15 Hz — deliberately narrower than the monitoring band. Centred on QRS energy, it actively suppresses P and T waves so they cannot be mistaken for beats.
  2. Differentiate. The QRS has the steepest slope in the signal; differentiation converts "steep" into "large".
  3. Square. Makes all values positive and amplifies large ones non-linearly, widening the margin between QRS and everything else.
  4. Moving-window integration over roughly 150 ms — approximately QRS width — converting spikes into one smooth hump per beat. Too narrow yields multiple peaks per complex; too wide merges adjacent beats.
  5. Adaptive dual thresholds track running estimates of signal and noise peaks, so amplitude drift needs no manual tuning. If no peak appears within 166% of the running average RR interval, the algorithm searches back at a lower threshold before declaring a miss.
  6. Physiological constraints. A 200 ms refractory period forbids two beats closer together than cardiac physiology allows. T-wave discrimination rejects candidates arriving 200–360 ms after a confirmed beat with a shallower slope.

RR intervals follow, and from them heart rate and HRV (RMSSD, SDNN, pNN50). The algorithm is worth implementing once to understand it, then replacing with a maintained library such as NeuroKit2 for production.

1.5Signal quality index — the highest-value output

For an episodic, spot-check recording, the most valuable thing the pipeline can produce is a trustworthy judgement of whether a recording is interpretable at all. An incorrect heart rate is far worse than an honest refusal to report one.

  • Kurtosis — clean ECG is spiky and high-kurtosis; noise is Gaussian and sits near 3.
  • In-band power ratio — energy in 5–15 Hz relative to total.
  • Detector agreement — run two different R-peak detectors and compare. They agree on real beats and diverge on noise. Cheap, and remarkably effective.
  • IMU motion energy — gates trust during movement.
  • Skin conductance — a direct proxy for contact quality (§3.3).

These fuse into a single score. The rejection rate it produces should be reported as a primary result: for a novel form factor, how often the device honestly declines to answer is the finding.

Part 2 Atrial fibrillation detection

2.1The physiology

In normal sinus rhythm the sinoatrial node fires regularly, producing a P wave then a QRS complex, with RR intervals that are regular apart from gentle respiratory modulation.

In atrial fibrillation the atria fibrillate chaotically at 300–600 bpm, and the atrioventricular node conducts impulses through essentially at random. Two signatures result:

  1. RR intervals become irregularly irregular — variable with no discernible pattern. This signature is robust and survives noise.
  2. P waves vanish, replaced by fibrillatory waves. Diagnostically definitive, but requiring clean morphology that a noisy single lead may not deliver.

We build on the first. The second demands signal quality this form factor may not support.

2.2The classical baseline

Over a window of roughly 30–120 consecutive RR intervals:

  • RMSSD — root mean square of successive differences; elevated in AF.
  • Normalised RMSSD — divided by mean RR, removing heart-rate dependence. This matters: raw RMSSD rises at low heart rates regardless of rhythm.
  • Shannon entropy of the binned RR distribution — AF is high-entropy.
  • Sample entropy — an irregularity measure robust on short records.
  • Poincaré SD1/SD2 — plotting each RR interval against the next, sinus rhythm forms a tight ellipse along the identity line while AF forms a diffuse cloud.

Thresholded, or fed to a small classifier, this is a genuine and defensible baseline that runs on-device in microseconds.

Its failure mode is what must be measured

Frequent ectopic beats also produce irregular RR intervals and will drive false positives — as will every missed or spurious R-peak from §1.4. The false-positive rate is therefore downstream of R-peak accuracy, which is downstream of signal quality. The chain is the product.

2.3Machine learning, only after the baseline exists

Training data would come from the PhysioNet/CinC 2017 challenge set — single-lead, four classes including an explicitly noisy category, making it the closest public analogue to our use case. Architectures should stay small enough to run on a phone.

Subject-wise splits are non-negotiable. A 2025 systematic review found that only 30.3% of published ECG classification studies used inter-patient splits, and only 4.1% met all evaluation criteria. The same review cites a study reporting 99.48% accuracy under intra-patient evaluation against 88.34% inter-patient on identical data. If our numbers resemble the literature's best, we should suspect leakage before celebrating.

Any model must beat the classical baseline on the same held-out split to earn inclusion. Frequently it will not — and that is a legitimate, publishable finding.

2.4What can and cannot be validated

We have no participants with atrial fibrillation. The claim therefore decomposes, and should be stated this way:

ClaimValidated byAvailable to us
Algorithm sensitivity to AFPublic annotated AF recordsYes
False-positive rate on our hardwareOur healthy cohort — any flag is falseYes
End-to-end clinical sensitivityPatients with AF wearing the ringNo

We therefore report algorithm sensitivity from public data and hardware false-positive rate from our own participants, separately. A combined "sensitivity and specificity for our ring" figure cannot be computed from what we have, and claiming one would be the fastest route to failing review.

Part 3 Multi-sensor integration

3.1Accelerometer and gyroscope

  • Reference channel for adaptive filtering (§1.3) — the primary use, and the main technical contribution of Month 2.
  • Signal-quality input — motion energy gates how far the ECG is trusted.
  • Activity context — suppressing arrhythmia flags during exercise, where elevated and variable heart rate is expected physiology. This alone removes a large share of false positives.

3.2Photoplethysmography

  • An independent second opinion on heart rate, derived from an entirely different physical mechanism — optical rather than electrical. Agreement between the two is a strong internal validity signal requiring no external reference.
  • Fusion for arrhythmia detection is well supported: signal-quality-weighted fusion has been reported to improve accuracy by 4.51% with two sensors and 10.92% with three at −10 dB SNR against single-input methods. The mechanism is complementary failure — ECG degrades from electrode noise, PPG from motion, and rarely both simultaneously.
  • Skin tone materially affects PPG, since melanin absorbs green wavelengths, but barely affects ECG, which is electrical. Any analysis across skin tones must treat the two arms separately.

3.3Skin conductance as a contact-quality sensor

Skin conductance is physically the same quantity that governs electrode-skin contact impedance. A sudden drop therefore predicts ECG degradation before it becomes visible in the ECG itself. Used as a leading indicator, it can drive both the quality index and a "reposition your finger" prompt. This is a genuinely novel angle for a ring form factor and merits its own section in any write-up.

3.4Temperature

Slow-moving and low-yield: useful for circadian baseline, contact detection and as a stress-index input. Worth capturing, not worth significant effort.

Part 5 Standing rules

  1. Validation is blocking, never skippable. An unvalidated filter is a filter we do not know works. If Track A slips, B and C stop — they are producing unfalsifiable output until it catches up.
  2. Subject-wise splits, always. Our numbers will look worse than much of the published literature. The literature is largely wrong on this point; we are not.
  3. Report sensitivity and positive predictivity together. Never bare accuracy — at 1% prevalence, a detector that always answers "no" is 99% accurate.
  4. Every user-visible number traces to a line in the validation report, not to an internal impression that something looked correct.
  5. Pre-register the Month 3 analysis plan before collecting Month 3 data. Cheap now, and it forecloses questions about post-hoc analysis later.
  6. Prefer honest failure to quiet fabrication. "Recording rejected, please retry" is always better than a confident wrong heart rate.

Verified during this analysis

The sensor's FIFO word format and status-tag semantics (manufacturer datasheet); the constant low-order bits across our own captures (arithmetic); the configured 128 SPS sample rate (library source); the systematic-review evaluation statistics; the sensor-fusion improvement figures.

Inferred rather than verified

  • A target of roughly 30 participants — a convention drawn from comparable studies, not a power calculation. A proper calculation should follow pilot data from about five participants.
  • That the ring's ECG is a two-handed spot check rather than continuous. Deduced from the physics of Lead I, which requires a potential difference across two limbs, and from how comparable ring devices operate — not from our schematic. This needs confirming, as the study protocol depends on it.
Non-invasive glucose — requires action

The evidence does not support a glucose feature. Regulators have issued an explicit safety communication warning against smartwatches and smart rings claiming non-invasive glucose measurement, and no such device has been authorised anywhere. A recent reproducibility study found that published PPG-glucose models collapse to near-zero or negative explained variance under participant-aware evaluation — no better than predicting the mean — while more than 90% of predictions still fall within clinically "acceptable" error-grid zones including the baseline, meaning the field's standard metric cannot distinguish a working model from nothing at all.

There is additionally no plausible optical mechanism at the wavelengths our sensor uses, which are chosen for haemoglobin rather than glucose. Reference glucose collection would also require fingerstick or continuous monitoring — invasive data collection, at a substantially higher ethics threshold than ECG.

Recommendation: retract or reframe the public claim now, and treat glucose as an explicitly negative-result research track rather than a roadmap feature. A well-executed negative replication is publishable and defensible; an unsupported claim is neither.
